Overview Basic Life Support

Basic life support is a type of emergency care and is an important skill for anyone who may encounter a cardiac or respiratory emergency in a setting where advanced medications and interventions are not initially availble. This could be at home, work or a public place such as a resturant or ball game. In these settings all you would have initially until helped arrived would be your skills and knowledge and if fortunate an AED/Debrillator and mabye naloxone (e.g. Narcan). Advanced life support is not addressed in this website.

The initial knowledge conveyed by the American Heart Association is in a series of algorithms. The main purpose of this website is to help you memorize these algorithms so that you can use them in an emergency situation by the use of memory systems. Of course there is much more to being able to effectively provide basic life support than just memorizing the algorithms. The section on Preparedness discusses this further.

Grouping of information

Before we strat using visual mnemonics to memorize the algorithms there is some initial preparation that can make the task more easier and more logical. Initially reading over these algorithms seems like a lot to absorb but after several readings you begin to see three things that we will initialy do to prepare: 1) The algorithms findings and actions can be placed in groups 2) Simplify wording and arrangement of the algorithms if it doesn't change the nature of the algorithm. 3) The algorithms have more components common than not

As I read over the algorithms I see all three can be divided into the following groups of findings and actions:

  • Initial Assesment
  • Stable Unrepsonsive Patient
    • Moniter
  • Respiratory Arrest
    • Ventilation Support
  • Cardiac Arrest
    • CPR

This grouping will be very helpful when we later use our memory palaces.

Simplify wording and arrangement

The best example is how I organized the initial assessment into the following:

  • Verify scene safety
  • Check for responsiveness
  • Shout for help
  • Call 911 (This is what I would actually say in real life rather than "Activate Emergency Response System" and it also leads to a good visual mnemonic of a cell phone with the numbers 911 on the screen.)
  • Get an AED/defibrillator
  • Get naloxone if possible opiod overdose (in the algorithms this was in sveral places which made the algorithm more complex. Moving it to the top simplifys the algorithm.)
  • Check for breathing and pulse but no more than 10 seconds
